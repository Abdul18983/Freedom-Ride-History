Stage 5 History Case Study: The 1965 Freedom Ride

A seven-lesson historical inquiry sequence focused on the 1965 Freedom Ride in New South Wales.

7 × 60-minute lessons Inquiry-based learning Primary + secondary sources ICT integrated Summative digital exhibition
Cultural notice: Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people should be aware that some linked historical collections and photographs may contain images, voices or names of people who have died.

Case Study Overview

This teaching sequence takes three weeks and focuses on the 1965 Freedom Ride in New South Wales as part of the Rights and Freedoms topic of Stage 5 History. The sequence consists of seven 60-minute lessons and employs an inquiry model to develop students' historical knowledge, source analysis and judgement skills.

The central inquiry question is:

To what extent did the 1965 Freedom Ride expose and challenge racial discrimination against Aboriginal Australians?

Throughout the sequence, the inquiry question will be revisited so that students can adjust their original ideas in light of new evidence. This will help students to learn history as a process of investigation.

The 1965 Freedom Ride is an appropriate case study as students will be able to work with many primary and secondary sources including photographs, diaries, newspaper reports, campaign material, oral histories, maps and museums' resources. It will also give students opportunities to explore different perspectives and the relationship between activism, media, discrimination and social change.

Syllabus Outcomes

The teaching sequence covers several Stage 5 History outcomes. Students explain continuities and change, investigate the motives and actions of individuals and groups, use evidence to explain the past, select and analyse historical sources, apply historical terms and concepts and communicate their historical knowledge and understanding in writing, visually and digitally.

After this sequence, students will be able to explain causes and purposes of the Freedom Ride, explore different perspectives on the campaign, evaluate the usefulness and limitations of sources, compare evidence, correlate evidence and construct evidence-based historical judgements.

Seven-Lesson Sequence

Lesson 1: What Can One Photograph Tell Us?

In this lesson, the unit will be introduced through historical inquiry rather than teacher explanations. Students will be shown one photograph of the Student Action for Aborigines bus with limited historical context.

The following See–Think–Wonder activity will be done by the students. They will note down what they see in the image, what they think might happen in it and what questions the image raises. After that, the students will discuss possible meanings of the image in groups and share their questions through a digital platform such as Padlet or Mentimeter.

The teacher will give the students brief information about racial discrimination and segregation of Aboriginal people during that time. At the end of the lesson, students will write an initial response to the main inquiry question. This response will be revised in the final lesson. The activity will develop students' skills of observation, inference and questioning.

Lesson 2: Why Did the Freedom Riders Leave Sydney?

In this lesson, causes and motivations of the Freedom Ride will be explored by the students.

Students will move around source stations and explore various pieces of evidence about the 1965 Freedom Ride such as photographs, newspaper extracts, campaign material, survey evidence and extracts from Ann Curthoys' diary.

In each station, students will complete a source analysis scaffold where they will identify the source's origin, purpose, historical context, perspective, usefulness and limitations. Finally, students will work in pairs or groups and use Canva or Miro to create a cause-and-effect diagram based on the analysis of the sources.

This lesson will help students to see that history is constructed through interpreting historical evidence. Students will compare the sources and evaluate whether the different types of evidence construct the same or different historical explanations.

Lesson 3: Mapping the Freedom Ride

In this lesson, the geographic reach of the Freedom Ride and differences between regional communities will be examined.

In groups, students will explore particular towns visited by the Freedom Riders including Wellington, Walgett, Moree, Bowraville and Kempsey.

Each group will find out the discrimination in the particular town, the protests that took place there, the local responses and the historical evidence about the event.

Then students will collaboratively create a digital map using Google My Maps or StoryMapJS. In the map, each place will be accompanied by a short description and historical evidence.

The lesson will help students to develop their spatial awareness, comparative thinking and contextual knowledge. The use of ICT technologies will allow organising historical evidence.

Lesson 4: Moree – Reconstructing a Historical Event

Lesson 4 will focus on the confrontation between Freedom Riders and the Moree residents as an example of a historical inquiry.

Students will compare several sources about the event including contemporary diaries, newspaper reports, photographs and recollections of participants.

In groups, students will reconstruct the sequence of events. Then they will complete a corroboration activity where they will identify agreements between the sources, contradictions and the information gaps.

Finally, students will write a brief response in which they explain which sources are more useful for reconstructing the event and why.

Through this lesson, students will learn that sources cannot be simply classified into reliable and unreliable. The sources should be evaluated in terms of usefulness depending on its purpose, perspective and context.

Lesson 5: Did the Media Make the Freedom Ride Powerful?

In this lesson, the role of media coverage of the campaign will be explored by the students.

Students will compare the sources about Freedom Ride, including newspaper articles, photographs and participant recollections and will discuss how the Freedom Ride was covered by the media and presented to the wider public.

Then, a structured class discussion will take place when students will debate the question whether journalists reported the events or whether media attention became an important part of the strategy of the campaign.

Students will create a mock newspaper front page or a short news report using Canva or Adobe Express. The report should be based on historical evidence rather than invention.

Also, students will write a brief reflection about which elements are based on sources and which require historical interpretation.

Lesson 6: How Significant Was the Freedom Ride?

In this lesson, students' understanding of historical significance will be developed.

Students will get evidence cards that are connected with different consequences of the Freedom Ride such as media attention, local changes, increase in public awareness and Freedom Ride within broader history of Aboriginal activism.

The students will do a diamond-ranking activity where they will rank the evidence according to their historical significance.

There is no right answer for the task. However, the students will need to justify their choices with evidence.

The class discussion will focus on the difference between immediate consequences and significance of the Freedom Ride. Students will avoid simplifications and will think about the Freedom Ride in the context of broader history of Aboriginal activism.

Lesson 7: Historical Inquiry Workshop

In this lesson, students will go back to their initial responses written in Lesson 1.

They will compare their original ideas with their current knowledge and will see what they have changed, what they kept and what they rejected because of the evidence.

First, the teacher will demonstrate the students how to construct a historical argument through making a claim, supporting it with evidence and explaining its significance.

Then, students will start working on the summative assessment task. Peer feedback will be used for evaluation of argument, evidence, source analysis and historical communication before the final submission of the assignment.

Summative Assessment Task

Summative assessment task for the teaching sequence is a digital historical exhibition called:

The 1965 Freedom Ride: Protest, Evidence and Change

The task is to create an 800-1000 word digital historical exhibition addressing the following question:

To what extent did the 1965 Freedom Ride expose and challenge racial discrimination against Aboriginal Australians?

The students will develop a clear historical argument and will use at least four primary sources and two credible secondary sources. Also, they will analyse at least two primary sources, include different perspectives, discuss both immediate and longer-term consequences of the Freedom Ride, will use appropriate historical terminology and will create source captions and a bibliography.

Photographs, maps, audio and video files may also be used in the exhibition if they make sense to the historical story.

Marking Criteria

The assessment is graded out of 20.

Out of six points, students will be assessed on their historical knowledge and argument. High-achieving students will construct an accurate and well-supported historical judgement addressing the inquiry question and demonstrating a good knowledge of the context and significance of the Freedom Ride.

Out of six points, students will be assessed on historical inquiry and source analysis. High-achieving students will analyse and correlate a varied range of sources while evaluating perspective, usefulness and limitations of the sources.

Out of four points, students will be assessed on their understanding of historical concepts and perspectives. They will show understanding of the concepts such as cause, change, significance and perspective. Also, they will incorporate different historical perspectives.

Out of four points, students will be assessed on their organisation, historical terminology, referencing and the purposeful use of digital features.

Teaching Resources, ICT and Differentiation

During the teaching sequence, a variety of resources will be used. Among primary sources, there will be photographs, diaries, campaign material, newspaper reports and oral histories. Secondary sources will include materials from historical institutions and museums.

ICT will be integrated when it facilitates historical inquiry. For example, Padlet and Mentimeter will be used for generating questions, digital mapping will be used for developing spatial understanding, Canva and Miro will be used for organising evidence and digital tools will be used for communicating historical interpretations.

Differentiation will be provided through simplified source extracts, vocabulary support, sentence starters, source analysis scaffolds and teacher modelling. Multimodal resources will provide different access points for the students. Extension students will be able to compare the Australian Freedom Ride with the United States Freedom Rides or explore different interpretations of its significance.

Cultural responsiveness is also very important. Aboriginal Australians should not be simply represented as passive targets of the student activism. The voice of the Aboriginal people should be heard as much as possible. Also, students should be aware about perspectives that are well-represented in archival collections and about those that may be hidden.
